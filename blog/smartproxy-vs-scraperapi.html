Every architecture review about scraping infrastructure eventually reaches the same fork, and it is usually argued badly. One engineer wants to buy residential bandwidth from Smartproxy (Decodo) and drive it with curl_cffi or Playwright. Another wants to point a single HTTP call at ScraperAPI and let someone else own the anti-bot problem. Both are described as "the scraping vendor", both have a monthly bill, and the argument ends up comparing a per-gigabyte number against a per-month number — which is not a comparison at all.

The two products are not substitutes. One is a transport: 115M+ real-user IPs across 195+ locations, billed by the gigabyte, with the client, the fingerprint, the retry policy and the challenge logic entirely your problem. The other is a service: you hand it a URL, it returns HTML, and the pool, the browser, the CAPTCHA handling and the retry loop are inside a black box you rent by the credit.

That difference produces two different cost curves, and they cross. Below is the arithmetic for where they cross, three scenarios on either side of the crossing, and the routing pattern that most production teams end up with — because it is usually cheaper than committing to either product for 100% of traffic.

The fork in the road: two products, two invoices

The question an architect actually has to answer is not "which vendor is better". It is: how much of my traffic is hard, and how expensive is my own engineering hour? Everything else follows from those two numbers.

The cost model on both sides

I use the same model that ships in the public true-cost calculator, so every number below is reproducible by moving the sliders. On the DIY side:

retry_multiplier      = 1 / (1 - block_rate)
bandwidth_factor      = 1 + (block_rate / (1 - block_rate)) * 0.35
js_payload_multiplier = (1 - js_share) + js_share * 2.4
avg_kb_per_page       = html_kb * js_payload_multiplier * bandwidth_factor
real_gb               = pages * avg_kb_per_page / 1,048,576
proxy_cost            = real_gb * price_per_gb
compute_cost          = pages * js_share / 100,000 * $18
engineering_cost      = hours_per_month * $75
DIY_total             = proxy_cost + compute_cost + engineering_cost

Two constants carry most of the argument. The 0.35 is the share of a challenge response that still gets billed as bandwidth: you paid to be told no. The 2.4× is the payload inflation of a headless render once scripts, fonts, images and XHR traffic are counted — a 250 KB page becomes roughly 600 KB of metered traffic.

On the managed side the model is a credit count and a tier lookup:

credits = pages * ((1 - js_share) * 1 + js_share * 5)
cost    = tier_for(credits)   # 49 / 149 / 299 / 475 / ... / linear

The 5-credit JS multiplier is the calculator's planning assumption, not a vendor quote. ScraperAPI's own documentation puts a plain page at 1 credit and a Cloudflare/DataDome bypass at +10 credits, so 5 is deliberately conservative for a rendering-heavy mix and optimistic for a protected one. Every table below states which multiplier it used.

Where this scenario breaks

Push the block rate to 68.7% and the DIY total crosses $299 at this page size, because bandwidth alone doubles. Push the HTML to 60 KB and the pool bill rises to $267.31 — still cheaper than $299, but the margin is now a single engineer-hour. And the moment your targets start answering with Turnstile instead of a page, the block rate is not 8%, it is 35% or worse, and you are in scenario 2.

The managed API is 74% cheaper — well past the "net saving over 65%" threshold that makes the decision non-negotiable. Three multipliers did it, and none of them is a discount:

  • Retry amplification. A 35% block rate means 1.538 billed attempts per usable page, and on a residential meter the failures are paid in bytes just like the successes.
  • Payload inflation. Headless rendering multiplies metered traffic by 2.12× at an 80% JS share. You are paying residential prices for fonts, images and analytics beacons you will never parse.
  • Engineering. Eight hours a month at $75 is $600 — more than the entire managed plan. This is the line item that never appears in the DIY column of a vendor comparison, and it is usually the decisive one.

Sensitivity matters more than the headline. Drop maintenance to two hours a month and DIY falls to $711.62: still 2.4× the managed bill, still losing. Raise it to eight hours a week (34.7 h/month) and DIY reaches $3,164.12 — 10.6× the managed bill. Drop the block rate to 20% and the pool still costs $1,116.96 at these page sizes, because bandwidth — not retries — dominates once rendering is involved. The only way this scenario flips back to the pool is a much cheaper per-GB rate or a much smaller rendered payload, and neither is under your control at a protected target.

Where this scenario breaks

Two places. First, credit multipliers: if every one of those 250k pages sits behind Cloudflare, the real bill is not 5 credits per rendered page but 11 (1 standard + 10 bypass). That is 2.75M credits — still inside the $299 Business tier's 3M allowance, but one more multiplier notch (12+ credits per page) puts you at 3M+ and the next plan is $475. Second, freshness: a managed API's cache and retry behaviour is not yours to tune, so a workload that needs a guaranteed cold fetch at a specific second may not be serviceable at any credit count.

Scenario 3 — the hybrid fallback pattern that beats both

Here is the pattern most teams converge on after paying for both mistakes once. Route every request through the cheap path first, and escalate only the ones that actually fail. The pool handles the 95% of traffic that never needed a bypass; the managed API handles the 5% that did.

Model: 1,000,000 pages a month, of which 950,000 are light (40 KB, 5% block, no rendering) and 50,000 are hard (140 KB, 45% block, 90% rendering). Half an hour of operations on each leg.

If you have to touch the router every week, the saving evaporates and the managed-everything architecture was the correct answer.

The 30-line router, Python standard library only

This is the whole pattern — thirty non-blank lines, standard library only. It tries the residential pool, inspects the response for the two signals that mean "this was not a page" (a challenge status code or a challenge marker in a small body), and escalates exactly those requests to the managed API. Everything is urllib, json and environment variables — no SDK, no framework, no database.

import json, os, sys, urllib.error, urllib.parse, urllib.request

PROXY, SCRAPE = os.environ["RESI_PROXY_URL"], os.environ["SCRAPERAPI_KEY"]
MARKERS = ("cf-chl", "turnstile", "Just a moment", "captcha-delivery")
CHALLENGE_STATUS = {403, 429, 503}

def _get(url, proxy=None, timeout=30):
    handler = urllib.request.ProxyHandler({"http": proxy, "https": proxy} if proxy else {})
    req = urllib.request.Request(url, headers={
        "User-Agent": "Mozilla/5.0 (Windows NT 10.0; Win64; x64) AppleWebKit/537.36 "
                      "(KHTML, like Gecko) Chrome/124.0.0.0 Safari/537.36",
        "Accept-Language": "en-US,en;q=0.9"})
    with urllib.request.build_opener(handler).open(req, timeout=timeout) as r:
        return r.status, r.read().decode("utf-8", "replace")

def fetch(url):
    """Residential pool first; escalate only when the answer was not a page."""
    try:
        status, body = _get(url, proxy=PROXY)
        if status not in CHALLENGE_STATUS and not (
                len(body) < 20000 and any(m in body for m in MARKERS)):
            return body, "residential"
    except (urllib.error.URLError, TimeoutError):
        pass                              # a proxy failure is itself an escalation
    endpoint = ("https://api.scraperapi.com/?api_key=%s&url=%s"
                % (SCRAPE, urllib.parse.quote(url, safe="")))
    status, body = _get(endpoint)
    if status != 200: raise RuntimeError("%s: HTTP %s on both paths" % (url, status))
    return body, "managed"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for target in sys.argv[1:]:
        page, path = fetch(target)
        print(json.dumps({"url": target, "path": path, "bytes": len(page)}))

One note on using it in anger: urllib presents a Python TLS fingerprint, which is precisely the failure mode described in the sticky-session and JA3 teardown; in production swap _get for curl_cffi.requests.get(..., impersonate="chrome") and the escalation rate drops immediately. Measure the escalation rate before and after — that single number tells you whether you are paying for the managed API out of necessity or out of a client bug.

The decision rule, in one paragraph

Buy the pool when the payload is small, the block rate is under roughly 10%, and someone already owns the client — the arithmetic gives you a 2–4× cost advantage that survives volume growth. Buy the managed unblocker when rendering is unavoidable, more than a third of your targets answer with a challenge, and the engineering time to keep a stealth stack alive is a real line item on someone's calendar. Buy both when the traffic is mixed, because the hybrid routes the cheap majority through the cheap meter and confines the expensive product to the requests that actually need it — and check the escalation rate monthly, since that percentage is the only input that decides whether the hybrid still pays for itself.

None of this is a prediction about your success rate. It is arithmetic over four numbers you can measure this week: pages per month, average HTML size, first-attempt block rate, and the JS share of your targets. Get those four right and the vendor question answers itself.
